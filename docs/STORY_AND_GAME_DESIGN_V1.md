# THIEF OF DAWN
## Story & game design — Version 1.0

**Status:** Creative proposal for review. Nothing below has been added to Umbraverse canon or implemented in the Godot repository.

**Genre:** Narrative-driven 2D action-stealth platformer, not a Metroidvania.
**World:** Umbraverse, Era of Dawn.
**Story years:** 320, 324, 329, and 335.
**Scope:** One substantial game (target 15–20 hours of main story, with additional optional jobs), four playable life periods, roughly 22 authored stages grouped into four chapters. The first chapter can be completed and enjoyed on its own.

> **Logline:** A teenage Norran thief escapes a provincial holding cell with no plan and no destination. His flight carries him into a Verdan forest he never knew existed. Over fifteen years, every shortcut he takes to survive creates another debt—to his family, his companions, and the strangers he steals from. He never saves Umbraverse. He must eventually decide whether the people he calls his own are worth staying for when running would be easier.

> **Guiding rule:** Renn matters enormously to a handful of people, and almost not at all to history.

---

## 1. Canon boundaries and proposals

**Existing canon used without alteration:** The Era of Dawn spans Years 0–500; Norra was founded in Year 100; its Norran people have blue skin and cultures of mining, practical engineering and craftsmanship. Humans come in five ethnicities. Verdans are plantlike humanoids with affinities for forests, plant growth and natural environments. The Grand Library of Thalor (Year 1500), the Order of the Shield (1650), the Grand Festival (1600), and the Council of Five (2000) do **not** yet exist. Neither the Great War nor the later human alliance politics has happened.

**Invented for this story, all draft:** Slateford and its economy; Renn and his relatives; Hadrik, Telu, Iri, Deka, Suli and Orven; the Greenwake forest and its local Verdan community; Years 285–320 micro-history; the local legal and restitution practices; Verdans' local boundary markers; every heist, institution and event in these chapters. This is a proposed **local** relationship between a few humans and a specific Verdan community, not a declaration about relations between the species everywhere.

**Design stance:** No FATE Marks, prophecy, royal plot, ancient artefact, universe-ending threat, legendary criminal organization, guaranteed redemption, or special combat destiny. The protagonist never encounters non-humans before his flight in Year 320.

## 2. The protagonist

### Renn — Norran human

- **Year 320:** age **15**. Slate-blue skin, metallic-bronze hair, dark onyx eyes: consistent with documented Norran appearance. Short, wiry, scraped knuckles, practical hand-me-down clothes. Later costumes reflect wear, not elaborate hero armour.
- **Birth:** Year 305, Slateford, an invented Norran satellite settlement in the Northern Mountains' foothills.
- **Family:** Ilen (mother; rope-maker), Boran (father; cart repairer), and Nera (sister, age 12 in Year 320). All are alive when the story begins. They're neither abusive caricatures nor secretly aristocratic.
- **Education:** Cannot read. Can count coins, recognize faces, judge weight, remember routes, identify rope knots, imitate signatures poorly, and recognize repeated visual symbols. Do **not** conflate illiteracy with low intelligence.
- **Personality:** Observant, quick with a cutting joke, impulsive, defensive when called selfish, and genuinely excited when he successfully deceives someone. He can be affectionate one minute and steal from that same person the next. He wants comfort, respect and freedom without paying the ordinary price for them.
- **Strength:** Fast improvisation; physical courage in short bursts; reading people's attention; climbing, scrambling and slipping through tight places.
- **Flaw:** He treats other people's trust as an exploitable resource. The easiest way out is usually the one he takes—even when someone else pays later.
- **Why he steals:** It began as opportunism among children at markets. Earning a day's wage felt slower and more humiliating than lifting a day's wage from an inattentive trader. Circumstance made his life precarious, but **choosing theft is his own responsibility**.
- **What he wants, underneath the immediate wants:** To make his own decisions and have somewhere he can return to without being told what he owes. He doesn't articulate that at fifteen; the player sees it through his behaviour.
- **Character arc:** From someone who always runs before considering whom he leaves behind, to someone who occasionally accepts a dangerous obligation because people—not possessions—have become part of his self-interest. This is **limited growth**, not moral purification. He remains a thief.

### How he talks

Renn avoids speeches. He deflects embarrassment with humour, answers accusations by correcting small factual details, and apologizes only when the alternative is more expensive.

> **Guard:** “Three market thefts.”
>
> **Renn:** “Two. Third one was from the cart.”
>
> **Guard:** “That isn't better.”
>
> **Renn:** “Wasn't trying to make it better.”

## 3. The world during Renn's childhood

### Slateford: a settlement history small enough to be believable

- **Year 285 (draft):** Norran wagon repairers, quarry hands and rope-makers establish a service settlement called **Slateford** on a minor ore-cart route outside Norra. The settlement is not another great city.
- **Year 301 (draft):** A local slope failure damages storehouses and several homes. Families relocate into crowded workshops on the lower road. This is *localized displacement*, not a continent-spanning migration.
- **Year 311 (draft):** Flood damage diverts some wagon traffic, squeezing the livelihoods of tradespeople whose income depends on passing carts. Many still do well; not everyone is impoverished.
- **Year 314–319 (draft):** Renn learns pickpocketing and fences small stolen goods. He sometimes gives money to his household and sometimes spends it on himself. His parents know enough to disapprove, but his mother has covered restitution once before.
- **Year 319 (draft):** After repeated thefts and a damaged cart incident, Ilen pledges the family rope-twisting frame as security for a restitution agreement. Renn knows the frame is at risk. No universal law about collective family punishment is implied: this is a **specific agreement Ilen signed**.
- **Year 320:** Renn is caught stealing again. A local hearing assigns several months of supervised road work. He escapes the holding cells before transfer. Because he absconds while the earlier guarantee remains outstanding, the pledged frame is seized; his family pays a genuine cost for his choice.

The settlement has a watch, a small holding room, a weighing house, a roadworks crew, a market and enough foot traffic for petty theft. It does **not** possess the institutions established centuries later.

### Greenwake (proposed Verdan forest)

A mature forest beyond the better-known human work roads, with rain-fed root channels, living bridges and cultivated tree nurseries. **Greenwake** and its community are new draft lore. One local Verdan, **Telu**, maintains water routes. Telu is a capable individual with obligations and a community; Verdans are not generic mystical forest guardians and are not default enemies.

The story establishes only that **this community knows humans sometimes come near its boundary and does not welcome damage or theft**. Whether Verdans elsewhere have regular dealings with humans remains unresolved.

## 4. Main characters and relationships

| Character | Initial identity | Individual interest | Relationship with Renn |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ilen** | Norran rope-maker; Renn's mother | Keep her trade alive and Nera secure | Loves Renn, but stops covering his losses. She is allowed anger without becoming cruel. |
| **Boran** | Norran cart repairer; Renn's father | Honest work, enough predictable income | Distrusts his schemes; refuses to make every conversation about morality. |
| **Nera** | Renn's younger sister, 12 in Year 320 | Wants an adult life not dictated by Renn's mess | Starts admiring his cleverness; grows up resenting the cost. Later becomes literate and takes responsibility for the family business. |
| **Hadrik** | Local watchman, not a commander or conspirator | Recover a repeat offender and save professional face | Pursues Renn over several days because Renn escapes publicly and injures his pride. His jurisdiction, time and stamina are finite. His role ends in Chapter I; later appearances, if any, are local and conditional. |
| **Telu** | Verdan caretaker of a Greenwake water-route and nursery | Keep the channel and young trees alive | First sees Renn as a damaging trespasser. Their relationship remains conditional and transactional; time allows it to change. |
| **Iri** | Itinerant Thalorian tally-worker and adept fraudster | Earn money, keep leverage, control risk | Literate and perceptive; Renn wants her skills but hates relying on her. First ongoing accomplice. |
| **Deka** | Norran wheelwright and occasional thief | Escape mounting local debts and find work on his own terms | The partner who expects Renn to mean his promises. His later arrest forces the adult Renn's final decision. |
| **Suli** | Dornith performer and market con artist | A reliable share of the proceeds and independence | Excellent at distraction; refuses to confuse criminal partnership with a family obligation. |
| **Orven** | Valerin caravan escort who takes illegal jobs | Money and room to work outside rigid expectations | Can win ordinary fights Renn cannot; blunt about the consequences of violence. **Not** a member of the later Order of the Shield. |

These people have their own lives. The five thieves are **Renn, Iri, Deka, Suli and Orven**, assembled slowly; they never formally proclaim a guild. The age, arrival and departure of companions should feel earned rather than arranged to complete a set of five.

## 5. The complete narrative spine

### Chapter I — THE BOY: **No Road Back**
**Year 320 · Renn age 15 · target 3–4 hours · a complete escape story with its own ending**

**Immediate want:** Avoid the road-work sentence and avoid being caught again.
**Opposition:** A working holding cell, his own impulsiveness, a local watchman, exposure, hunger, and an unfamiliar community defending its territory.
**Moral cost:** Running forfeits the family workshop equipment his mother knowingly pledged.

1. **A familiar offence.** A brief playable market sequence shows Renn stealing repeatedly, including a second theft he does not need. He is recognized, chased, and caught. He jokes until he sees that Ilen has come to the hearing. The sentence is mundane, harsh and local.
2. **The holding cells.** The player learns his body: climb, ledge-hang, slide, hide, throw a pebble. An improvised chance lets him slip free before dawn. There is no magical key, secret mission or cellmate who turns into his true purpose. In escaping, he knocks Hadrik into a railing, humiliating him before the other guards.
3. **Through Slateford.** Renn takes clothes and food from others to evade the search. On the market roofs he sees workers hauling the pledged rope frame away from his family's workshop. He briefly hesitates. He does not go back. Nera sees him from the lane; he makes a dismissive gesture and keeps running. The player understands both his freedom and its cost.
4. **The old work road.** Rain, a washed-out crossing, terraces and a deserted mill create a chase that alternates short confrontations and difficult movement. Hadrik stays on his trail because he can reasonably follow a wounded fugitive over a few days; he is not an unstoppable supernatural hunter.
5. **The living boundary.** Renn enters Greenwake because it seems a good place to vanish. Signs and braided plant markers mean nothing to him. He steals a practical pruning hook to make shelter and cuts a woven-root line, unknowingly blocking water to a nursery. Telu finds the damage, not a prophecy. Their first encounter is tense, physical and unintelligible.
6. **A debt paid in labour.** Telu directs Renn toward the damaged channel with gesture and demonstration. He tries to flee, but heavy rain floods the lower passage and the only dry exit crosses the disrupted system. Fixing the waterway is the **practical price of getting himself out**. Hadrik follows into the forest and becomes trapped at a damaged bridge. Renn can pull him clear at personal cost or keep moving while Telu's community later handles the intruder. Neither choice makes Renn saviour or monster. A second choice—return a stolen tool or conceal it—changes Telu's memory of him.
7. **First dawn.** The water flows; the forest community allows Renn one night's shelter outside its nursery and points him toward a path beyond the watch's reach. Hadrik stops pursuing him. Renn is free **now**. His family has lost the frame. He has no money, no home he can safely enter, and no idea where he'll go. A short, non-sentimental closing image shows Nera and Ilen starting work with inadequate tools. The escape arc is resolved; its consequences remain.

**Chapter I ending requirement:** A player who stops here has escaped custody, survived a dangerous border, met Verdans, faced a costly choice, and seen the consequences at home. It is a small but **complete** story, not a cliffhanger requiring a second instalment.

### Chapter II — THE WANDERER: **What a Road Costs**
**Year 324 · age 19 · target 3–4 hours**

**Immediate want:** Stop sleeping outdoors and acquire a safe room and steady enough money that he can choose his own risks.
**Opposition:** Tollkeepers, merchants, rivals, the physical cost of living from one stolen meal to the next, and his inability to read terms others use against him.

Renn has spent years moving through small work roads, taking seasonal jobs when convenient and stealing when convenient. There is no montage of him becoming a legendary outlaw. He is better at climbing, faster at recognizing danger, and no better at promises.

A collapsed river crossing traps him on the wrong side of a paying job. He attempts to pass off stolen goods and meets **Iri**, a Thalorian tally-worker who can read every crate mark and is herself adjusting weights to cheat a buyer. Each recognizes the other's dishonesty. They conspire to recover payment from an unreliable ferryman, but Iri misrepresents their agreement in writing. Renn catches the discrepancy **not by suddenly reading**, but by counting weights and watching what changes hands. Their shared caper nearly fails because neither trusts the other. They finally take payment and part ways, then discover they need another job together.

Mid-chapter Renn returns to the Greenwake boundary to stash goods. Telu recognizes him—and also his old damage. What Renn remembers as Verdan hostility proves to have been a warning to stop cutting a working water channel. Nobody explains the history of an entire species. Telu shows him a marker, then the downstream nursery it protects. He can respond defensively or accept that he understood nothing. The change is quiet: he is learning to **observe instead of only flee**.

**Chapter resolution:** Renn and Iri secure an unwanted, weatherproof room above a workshop through fraud and work, not luck. They have a base of operations and an uneasy agreement about money. He gets the one thing he wanted—a roof—but now must rely on somebody else to keep it.

### Chapter III — THE FIVE: **Five Hands**
**Year 329 · age 24 · target 5–6 hours**

**Immediate want:** Run jobs large enough to secure financial independence without submitting to a boss or honest trade.
**Opposition:** The complications of five self-interested people working together; victims who can fight back; a fence who changes prices; the reality that theft redistributes risk as well as coin.

Renn and Iri's ad hoc arrangement has acquired **Deka**, a Norran craftsman who knows doors and mechanisms; **Suli**, a Dornith performer who knows crowds; and **Orven**, a Valerin escort who can defend an exit. Each joined on a separate job because doing so made sense at the time. They exchange recognizable knots and chalk marks to signal safe passages and hidden stashes—useful because Renn cannot read. No charter, hierarchy or ceremonial guild founding occurs.

Early successes give players genuine satisfaction: planned multi-route heists, rooftop distractions, fence negotiations and narrow escapes. Renn grows overconfident. He takes a lucrative job to empty a local tally-house lockbox, believing the money belongs to a prosperous contractor. In fact, it includes a group of workers' withheld seasonal wages. The deception is not a secret artefact or conspiracy: the money is ordinary money, and real people need it.

The crew learns what was in the chest **after** the theft. Their fence has already moved part of it. Deka demands they compensate the workers because the retaliation will fall on families like his. Suli says they agreed to steal, not to conduct charity. Orven cares about exposure. Iri accuses Renn of ignoring warnings he didn't want to understand. Renn wants to keep the money and keep the crew: he cannot have both without paying something.

The latter half becomes an **inside-out heist**: recover the remaining wage money from their own fence while the offended workers, the tally-house guard and rival criminals all search for whoever stole it. The crew can return part, all, or none of the recovered money according to limited player decisions; each outcome has local, visible effects and alters relationships. The game does not attach a good/evil meter.

**Chapter resolution:** The heist crisis closes and the group survives, but the idea that they are simply five friends has cracked. They disperse to their own lives between jobs. Renn has learned to organize people, yet still mistakes obedience for loyalty.

### Chapter IV — THE MAN: **The Door That Stayed Shut**
**Year 335 · age 30 · target 4–6 hours · definitive game conclusion**

**Immediate want:** Get Deka out of custody and keep the last viable partnership from collapsing. He also wants—without saying so—to walk into Slateford and feel that he hasn't lost everything there.
**Opposition:** Local authorities with better records, his own past, the risk that using family property as cover will hurt Nera the way it hurt Ilen, and the remaining companions' unwillingness to sacrifice themselves for Renn.

Deka has been arrested on an ordinary theft in Slateford after a rushed job. Unlike fifteen-year-old Renn, he was **trying to get back to his companions** when he was caught. Deka knows names and stash locations; leaving him behind is both dangerous and a betrayal. Renn returns to the home settlement he has avoided for fifteen years.

Slateford has changed: roads repaved, shops inherited, slopes repaired unevenly, faces replaced. None of these changes is a historical milestone. **Nera**, now twenty-seven, knows contracts and runs a modest version of the family rope trade. Ilen has survived without Renn; her reception is neither a joyful reunion nor cartoon hatred. Nera knows why the rope frame was lost. When Renn offers stolen coin to set things right, she refuses to let him rewrite the past as a debt that can be paid in one visit.

Renn initially plans to use the family's workyard as a hiding place for the rescue. It is the easy option. Nera discovers the plan and tells him, clearly, that he will not put their livelihood up as collateral **again**. Renn has a choice in how he responds, but the central plot obliges him to find another route: to reach the holding cells without handing the risk to his family.

The final multi-stage burglary **mirrors the opening**, but in reverse. As a boy he broke **out** of a cell, alone. As a man he breaks **into** a cell and must leave with somebody who cannot move as fast as he can. Iri deciphers schedules and catches changes in a forged order; Suli distracts civilians on her own terms; Orven holds a passage at personal risk; Renn climbs the familiar prison structure and opens a route for Deka. None of them is compelled by destiny. Their motives overlap just enough.

At the climax, Renn's original shortcut—through the family yard—becomes available once again, now because a guard patrol has moved. It is the fastest exit. Taking it would expose Nera's workshop. Renn instead chooses a slower flooded service channel, risking his own escape and destroying his most profitable stash to distract pursuit. This decision is not moral sainthood. It is the first time he refuses to buy his freedom by making the same family pay twice.

The group escapes. Deka has his own reasons not to forgive Renn for the earlier wage heist, but he sees what has changed. Some of the five may continue working together; others depart. Their little system of knots and safe passages persists between people who know it. No one proclaims an order, no bard records their names, and the region's history does not pivot on the event.

**Ending:** Renn visits Ilen and Nera one final time. Nera does not invite him back to live there. Ilen does not perform a grand absolution. They talk, awkwardly, as adults. Outside the settlement he ties the familiar knot on a branch for the next traveler who knows the mark. The final image echoes the game's first dawn: once he ran with nobody to wait for; now he can run, but somebody might reasonably expect him to return. The last button press takes him **toward** his companions rather than away from them.

**Player-affected epilogues:** Whether Hadrik remembers rescue or abandonment; how Telu views Renn; whether the returned wages changed any workers' lives; which partners remain close. The central story resolves in every version. Renn never becomes a hero, king, prophet, chosen one, or famous founder.

## 6. The Verdan relationship: small, independent and revisitable

The Verdans must have agency and material lives. Greenwake is not a magical obstacle course populated by friendly quest-givers. Its local people cultivate, manage water, repair paths, argue and decide how far to trust outsiders.

**First meeting, Year 320:** Renn has never knowingly seen a non-human. Telu can identify him as an intruder without automatically interpreting everything he says. No exposition dump translates the whole culture. Plants physically react to Verdans because their existing lore supports control of growth; avoid turning all ordinary vegetation into a magic weapon.

**Later contact, Year 324:** Renn realizes a marker he took as a threatening emblem meant something like **water channel—do not sever** in that locality. This is not a universal Verdan alphabet canonization. Some misunderstandings remain. They may barter occasionally without becoming friends.

**By adulthood:** Telu's community remains its own story. If Renn returns, their response reflects his prior conduct. His mere presence has not united humans and Verdans. An optional final forest visit can reveal how much of his initial fear was ignorance and how much was justified caution on both sides.

## 7. Core game design

### 7.1 Player fantasy and loop

**Player fantasy:** Be a resourceful, physically ordinary thief who survives by movement, attention, deception and occasionally violence. The pleasure is not domination; it is the narrow escape you barely earned.

**Core loop:** Observe a location → select a route → steal, evade, distract or fight dirty → deal with the consequences → reach a temporary refuge → spend resources, speak with people, and choose the next job.

**Level structure:** Authored side-scrolling stages with alternate vertical routes, small optional secrets, human-scale settlements and natural hazards. No compulsory Metroidvania backtracking. Between levels, a small camp, room or safehouse supports conversation and gear selection; chapter transitions advance time.

### 7.2 Movement and combat

Retain the existing Godot prototype's foundation—weighty run, variable jump, ledge grab, climb, one-way platforms, camera and collisions—but tune it to a human thief. Wall jumping is a learned, plausible climbing technique if it remains; **no FATE Mark unlock** is required.

**Controls/verbs:** Run, jump, grab/climb, crouch, slide/roll, interact, shove/strike, throw a carried item, and switch a small number of tools. A contextual grab can pull a hanging object or drag a stunned person a short distance.

**Combat:** Renn is faster than most guards but weaker than trained fighters. A shove opens space; a kick or cheap shot briefly stuns; a found stick or knife is risky, not a power progression. An alert guard can beat him quickly, and two organized guards are dangerous. Quiet avoidance is viable, but the game should not punish every fight as a design failure. Orven later demonstrates why experience matters.

**Enemy intelligence:** Patrol → suspicion → investigation → chase → search → return. Sight, sound, lighting and remembered last position must be legible and testable. Guards cannot see through floors, instantly know Renn's location, or share information telepathically.

**Failure:** Checkpoint reset at the last safe spot/scene beat. Story decisions persist once committed at a chapter save, but ordinary level deaths never permanently undo character relationships. No punishing permadeath.

### 7.3 Stealing and equipment

Stealing uses authored spatial opportunities instead of random success rolls: distract a market seller, lift a latch from an exposed side, cut a purse only when unobserved, redirect a cart, access a barred box via the roof. Coins and loot buy food, basic equipment, safehouse fees and bribes; they never turn Renn into a magically upgrading warrior.

Carry only **two quick tools** and **one carried object** at once. Example tools: pebble/small noisemaker, rope, short pry hook, lantern, sling. Options are available by chapter and context, rather than a giant skill tree. An exhausted or empty-handed thief can still finish the story.

### 7.4 Illiteracy as experience, not a gimmick

- The ordinary game UI and subtitles remain readable for **player accessibility**. Renn himself cannot interpret written notices, legal papers or route labels.
- In-world writing is shown with consistent legible glyph art or overlays, while the protagonist's **interpretation** is sketch-like: road fork, cart, coin, warning symbol. No critical path depends exclusively on deciphering text.
- In Year 320, the local work-transfer notice has a picture of road tools; Renn misses the text specifying the transfer time. The player learns the deadline from overheard speech and context.
- In Year 324, Iri attempts to cheat Renn in a written agreement. He spots the fraud by measuring actual goods rather than suddenly learning letters.
- In Year 335, he can recognize a handful of repeated labels and names if established by scenes. This is incremental learning, not a sudden literacy miracle.
- Verdan markers pose **two** barriers: Renn cannot read even human script, and the Verdan medium/language is unfamiliar to most outsiders. Players learn local meanings through demonstration, pattern and consequences—not a universal magical translator.

### 7.5 Choices and consequences

Use **small, traceable relationship flags**, not a global morality score. A few named examples:

`hadrik_helped`, `telu_tool_returned`, `iri_share_honored`, `worker_wages_restored`, `nera_boundary_respected`.

Choices affect dialogue, a route, an optional helper, what happens to a secondary character, or the epilogue. They do **not** require four completely different final chapters. The story's moral ambiguity should come from credible trade-offs, not a flashing “good” versus “evil” badge.

### 7.6 Chapter-specific mechanical growth

| Chapter | New or deepened play | Narrative purpose |
|---|---|---|
| I. The Boy | Sprint, ledge grab, cover, simple distractions, weak shove, one Verdan environmental channel | Survive now; understand almost nothing. |
| II. The Wanderer | Bargaining, movable cargo, route observation, rope, first partner-assisted obstacle | Recognize that help and trust are separate. |
| III. Five Hands | Choose crew assistance per job, timed multi-route heists, thieves' signals, fence deals | Coordination makes bigger gains and bigger harms. |
| IV. The Man | Combine existing verbs in a prison infiltration; slower injured companion; returning changed environments | Demonstrate growth in action, not through an exposition speech. |

**Guard against feature creep:** The crew is not a four-character party RPG. Each job permits one authored partner interaction or distraction; companions aren't continuously pathfinding through the whole level. No open-world crime simulation, settlement management, real-time economy or procedural missions for this game.

## 8. Chapter I: playable level blueprint

**Six stages, roughly 25–45 minutes each, depending on exploration.** Each has a clear immediate objective and ends with a distinct tension/release beat.

| Stage | Location | Objective and play | Ending beat |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. The Market** | Slateford market stalls and wagon backs | Teach movement, observation and simple theft in a 15–20-minute prologue. Renn steals a second time by choice and is caught. | Mother arrives at hearing. No heroic misunderstanding. |
| **2. Behind the Door** | Timber-and-stone holding cell and guard walk | Escape using climb, crouch, weak shove, thrown pebble. Branch: roof vent or drain. | Hadrik is embarrassed and pursues. |
| **3. The Lower Roofs** | Workshops, awnings, ropes, narrow chimney tops | Outrun the street search; optional return to familiar alley costs time. | Renn sees the seized rope frame; chooses flight. |
| **4. The Cut Road** | Farmland, switchback, ditch, rain mill | A sustained chase, timed collapses, limited resources, occasional imperfect fights. | Renn runs into a forest he knows nothing about. |
| **5. Rootline** | Forest boundary, living root bridges, watercourse | Navigate unfamiliar markers, survive a Verdan encounter, discover Renn has broken an irrigation line. | Telu blocks his exit and shows the damaged nursery. |
| **6. Before the Light** | Wet nursery, raised channels, ravine bridge | Restore flow through platforming and environmental interactions, evade or save Hadrik, bargain for shelter. | First dawn: free beyond the district watch, but not welcome home. |

**The first playable vertical slice:** Stage 2 plus a compact version of Stage 3 (about 20–30 minutes) to test movement, stealth readability and improvised combat. **The first narrative playtest:** Stages 2–6 with a simple market prologue and complete ending. Don't wait for the adult-life assets to test Chapter I.

### Example opening scene

**Black screen.** Wheels outside. Renn counts footsteps: three pairs, then silence. A latch scrapes. A plain wooden door. The guard slides in a bowl of broth.

> **Hadrik:** “Don't make work for me today.”
>
> **Renn:** “Then open the door.”
>
> **Hadrik:** “You think you're funny.”
>
> **Renn:** “You keep coming back.”

When Hadrik leaves, the player notices a loose hinge, a drain opening and a length of thread under the bedding. Nothing glows. No prophecy is announced. The first challenge is simply to get through the door.

### Example first-act final scene

The nursery water runs again. The rain is softer. Telu places an unlit basket by the edge of a covered passage: food and a place to sit, but not an invitation deeper into the village. Renn tries to speak. Telu doesn't respond to the words. Renn eats, waits for another order, and finally sleeps.

**Cut to Slateford, before dawn.** Two adults haul a rope-twisting frame into a storehouse. Nera notices its missing wheel, then pushes a smaller, unfinished rope coil across the worktable to Ilen. Neither says Renn's name.

**Return to Renn.** He wakes as the sun clears the trees. He is alive, unchained, unwanted by the watch, and uncertain where to go next. **END CHAPTER I.**

| 18 | IV | **Slateford Again** | An older, altered hometown. The geography is recognizable but the social environment is not. |
| 19 | IV | **The Family Yard** | Try to arrange a safe entrance, face Nera and Ilen, and abandon the convenient harmful shortcut. |
| 20 | IV | **Two Doors** | Set up a coordinated infiltration; Iri's reading, Suli's distraction and Orven's strength all matter. |
| 21 | IV | **Into the Cell** | Mirror Level 2: enter confinement by choice and get an injured Deka out. |
| 22 | IV | **The Long Way Out** | Reject the family-yard exit; risk the flooded service route, lose a stash, and end at dawn. |

### Minimum authored systemic variations

- **Escape routes:** Each major settlement level has at least two traversable paths with different visibility, hazards and loot, not a fake choice that rejoins immediately.
- **Rescue / abandonment:** Hadrik's Chapter I incident changes a later local reference, but not the entire game map. Telu's attitude is responsive without being reduced to a reward dispenser.
- **The wage heist:** How much coin is returned changes the crew's conversations, workers' circumstances in one return visit, and the adult epilogue. The crew remains composed of the same named people up to the adult chapter.
- **The family:** Nera's trust is not a purchasable meter. Respecting her boundary in the final act affects whether she accepts a future visit, not whether the plot forces her to pardon Renn.

### Godot system boundaries for implementation

- **`PlayerController`**: movement, ledge, contextual shove, low-inventory interactions; should not know the current historical chapter.
- **`Awareness`/guard AI**: line of sight, sound response, last-known-position, patrol/search; enemies use the same signals regardless of stage.
- **`Interactable`**: loot, mechanism, carry/drag object, door, rope, vegetation channel; designers compose interactions without hard-coding a chapter number.
- **`StoryState`**: current chapter/year, five to ten explicit named persistent flags, a small relationship event log, local currencies/stashes; no generic alignment meter.
- **`Checkpoint`**: restores a stage snapshot on failure without multiplying story changes or rolling back committed decisions.
- **`ChapterTransition`**: time-skip montage, sprite/costume swap, new start node, persistent consequence checks.
- **`DiegeticText`**: distinguishes what is written in the world, what Renn understands, and what the player may access for accessibility.

**Vertical slice test matrix:** A playtester should finish Stage 02 with rooftop or drainage escape; evade one guard entirely or win one risky dirty fight; understand a guard's suspicion state; know that Renn is guilty, not falsely accused; and receive the same fair checkpoint treatment in either route.

## 10. Art, sound, tone and presentation

**Presentation:** 2D pixel art at the prototype's 640×360 logical resolution, Godot 4.7 compatibility. Expressive, grounded character animation. Cinematic scene composition from tiles and sprites, not expensive fully animated cutscenes.

**Visual contrast:**
- Slateford: grey stone, dusty timber, faded cloth, blue-skinned Norran workers and practical tools.
- Open country: harsh exposed ridges, cart roads, flood marks and sparse farms.
- Greenwake: layered greens, root-made structures with a lived-in logic, channels of moving water, luminous but **non-magical-looking** cultivated spaces unless justified by lore.
- Later stages: the same locations revisited with repaired walls, new signs, changed businesses and altered shortcuts. Time passing is visible through level geometry.

**Aging character art:** Use three strong visual silhouettes: teen (15), young adult (19–24), adult (30). Don't commission four wholly different animation libraries at the outset. Prioritize one base skeleton and compatible rigs where practical; scope and test before promising sprite reuse.

**Music:** Intimate, regional instrumentation and rhythm; no grand destiny choir. Pursuits use percussive motifs and shifting rhythm. The forest begins with unfamiliar textures, later becomes recognizable through repeated melody; the final chapter reprises the first prison rhythm with a different emotional register.

**Tone:** Wry dialogue, physical tension, occasional warmth, believable violence with consequences. Not relentlessly grim and not a comedy about charming harmless theft. People robbed by Renn have names, routines and wages.

## 11. Reuse vs rebuild: current Godot prototype

**Likely reusable after technical audit:** Godot project framework, pixel-perfect presentation, camera, ledge grab, climb, variable-height jump, rolling/sliding foundations, one-way platforms, hazard collision, checkpoints, scene transitions, audio routing, test harness, art workflows, and the content build pipeline.

Those aren't canonically appropriate for Year 320. Their prototype can remain a **technical reference** or a separate branch; do not erase it before the new direction is proven in a playable slice.

## 12. Ship plan and acceptance criteria

### Milestone 1: The playable thief (20–30-minute vertical slice)
One prison room, short yard and rooftop exit. Renn sprite; one guard with clear patrol/investigate/chase states; climb/slide/shove/throw; one theft interaction; gamepad support; scene checkpoint; readable feedback. **Pass:** A new player understands how to escape without a tutorial wall of text and can succeed using more than one route.

### Milestone 2: Chapter I complete and self-contained
Six authored stages, family cutscenes, Hadrik, Telu, a functioning Verdan nursery puzzle, consequence flags and a deliberate ending. **Pass:** A player can explain Renn's crime, his cost to the family, why the Verdans react, how the pursuit ends, and what Renn chooses at the climax. The ending feels satisfying without needing Chapter II.

### Milestone 3: Time skip and partner systems
Year 324 home base, Iri companion interactions and a small narrative heist. **Pass:** The time skip changes world, character and gameplay meaningfully without a second game's worth of mechanics.

### Milestone 4: Full life arc
Finish the crew chapter and adult return using existing mechanics in more demanding combinations. **Pass:** The final holding-cell infiltration tests everything learned, the family storyline gets a real conclusion, and choices affect specific people but not an unmanageable branching campaign.

### Scope guardrails
- Story-critical scenes must be authored; no procedural filler.
- Stage count is a production target, not a commitment to padding. Cut weak stages rather than manufacture plot.
- Only a handful of named NPCs receive repeatable dialogue and relationship flags.
- No full party-control mode, settlement simulation, sprawling crafting tree or early-world geopolitics exposition.
- **Do not canonize draft worldbuilding until reviewed.** Record source contradictions separately and never retroactively alter the five established peoples or chronology merely to support this plot.
